Can somebody explain why are there no V4 engines, and really what even is the difference between a V engine and a NON V engine...

Well,

engines have cylinders,

these cylinders can be placed in a straight line, therefore inline engines, or in two straight lines banked at an angle, which usually ranges from 180 degrees, in that case we're talking about "flat" engines, to about 45, except for fringe cases like Volkswagen's VR6 which has 15 degrees of bank

"Cool, but why are V4s not fit in cars?"

Basically the whole point of V shaped engines is that they're shorter than inline engines, that's why you never see anything bigger than an inline-6, V4 engines are just not that much shorter compared to inline 4s, on top of that they're more expensive and complicated, all for basically no reason.

"But why some bikes use them, then?"

Bikes need to be as balanced as possible, for obvious reasons so using an inline 4 in a bike would give it a higher center of gravity and make it wider because of how the engine sits in the frame, while a V4 is positioned in such a manner that it becomes NARROWER than an i4. That's also why it's used in MotoGP bikes and tends to win more than its inline counterpart. A pretty big downside is that they also cost more, but in racing application it's sort of irrelevant and on the high end market for street bikes, yeah pretty much the same.

Why is the Hemi, called Hemi? You ever wondered that? It's related to physics, because big Hemi V8s have an internal configuration that gives the combustion chamber a hemispherical shape, simple as that.

Now why would that be an advantage.

Regular engines have flat or wedge shaped combustion chambers, which leaves less space for the intake and exhaust valves, forcing to make em smaller, and since the sphere is the most efficient shape on surface area per volume ratio out of all the shapes The result is you can fit bigger intake and exhaust valves, which is the equivalent of fitting bigger lungs into your chest. Which means (mo power baby.gif) because more volume of air and fuel equals more explosion.

Little lore moment, Dodge introduced this tech in 1951 with the 426 HEMI, and it won so much that NASCAR got angry and banned it. ALSO, modern HEMIs don't have full hemispherical chambers anymore because... *cough* emission standards, but they keep the name because it's a massive money making asset.

But what's another thing modern HEMIs usually have?

Ah! Superchargers.

Very quickly they're a pair of big screws placed like this inside of a box, the screws spin because they're tied by a belt to the engine's crankshaft, and as they spin they suck in air which the screws compress and force feed down the engine's throat. This makes the engine produce more power instantly, BUT at a disadvantage, since the belt it tied to the crankshaft it steals a little bit of power from the engine to get things moving, the bigger the supercharger the bigger the power loss, but the added power from running a supercharger drastically compensates it.

A Turbochager on the other hand works by harvesting the exhaust flow, using it to spin a turbine, which spins another turbine, that sucks in fresh air, sending it through the intake and again force feeding the engine. But it too suffers from a problem. The infamous TURBO LAG, which basically happens because the exhaust flow is not strong enough to spin the turbine which then has to spin the other turbine and- you get the deal.

Basically the turbo can't force feed the engine until IT is in a high enough rpm band.

and to that engineers responded with ... anti-lag

Which works by keeping the throttle partially open even when you lift off the gas, that way some air enters and some combustion still happens, which generates some exhaust flow BUT when you need some aggressive flow, usually to spin up some massive turbo, the engine is tuned to retard ignition timings by a LOT, so that the explosion happens half where it needs to and half in the exhaust itself, and if you for SOME reason need even more than that, the ecu is tuned to run a really rich mixture of air and fuel, resulting in unburnt fuel that reaches the exhaust manifold, which makes explosions go off in it DIRECTLY, therefore creating a massive surge of exhaust flow.

All of this keeps the turbo spooled up and ready for when you step on the throttle again, but you understand that it is pretty detrimental for the engine itself... that's why they came up with an even better solution.

"Just chuck two turbos in there"

"Wouldn't that make the turbo lag WORSE?"

"Nah, hear me out bro"

We use a smaller turbo that gets spooled up by less exhaust flow for the lower rpm band and a bigger turbo for the higher rpm stages, therefore we cover all bases,

except that's WRONG ... (technically)

because you'd still leave the very low rpm band uncovered, which means you'd (in theory) lose in reaction times on an idle start to an equally powerful supercharged setup.

"Damn, i wonder if we could have both the benefits of a supercharged and a turbocharged setup in a single package"

You can, with a twin charged, but it's incredibly complicated and expensive, limiting its use to Group B, a few race cars and some Volvos

more in general ... i never understood the difference between BOLT-ON MODS and regular mods, so basically here's the rundown. Bolt ons are mods which all work AROUND the engine, exhausts, tunes, downpipes, stuff like that... even Turbos to a certain point, While NON BOLT ONS are mods to to engine internals, to avoid the engine going kaput with a 2000 horsepower ready sheepyrace twin turbo kit.

Really simply, if you have to work inside the engine to make the mods fit safely, it's not bolt ons anymore. So if somebody's making i dunno, 1000 horsepower out of their huracan with full bolt ons they're either lying in some way (a. you aint making 1000hp, b. you ain't on just bolt-ons)
